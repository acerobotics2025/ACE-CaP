Abstract

Code as Policies (CaP) offers a route for general-purpose coding agents to acquire physical skills through experience and retain them in executable programs. As an agent refines its policy through real-world interaction, a central challenge is to achieve reliable performance on challenging tasks while limiting the cost of physical experimentation. We investigate human guidance as a source of priors for feasible actions and simulation as an auxiliary environment for exploring and testing candidate policies. Our findings favor robot demonstrations available on request and simulation used selectively during real-world policy refinement. These findings inform ACE-CaP-0, an agentic recipe that couples policy revision with experience acquisition through Demonstrations on Request and Simulation on Demand. Across eight real-world manipulation tasks spanning alignment, multi-object handling, and bimanual coordination, the resulting frozen programs achieve 92.1% success (221/240 trials). Mean execution times are 0.97–2.27 minutes. The programs execute with runtime perception and control, without a human or coding agent in the loop. These results highlight CaP’s potential to turn experience into reliable, reusable physical skills.

What experience to acquire, and when

A study on charger insertion motivates the recipe. Six development conditions, one development run each, address three questions: what form of guidance to provide, when to provide it, and how to bring simulation into exploration. All use the same foundation model, task instruction, and interfaces, with at most ten human-assisted scene resets. Each resulting program is frozen and run for 30 real-world trials.

What form of guidance?

Three forms of assistance, each available when the agent asks for it, in development without simulation. Robot demonstrations give the highest final success, and both demonstration conditions take less development time and fewer output tokens than text alone.

  • Textual guidance

    60% 5.9 h, 0.86 M tokens

    Advice about manipulation strategies, action constraints, and corrections. No demonstration.

  • Robot-egocentric human video

    73% 3.8 h, 0.75 M tokens

    Text, plus videos of a person performing the task with their hands, recorded from the robot's execution viewpoint.

  • Robot demonstrations

    86% 3.7 h, 0.73 M tokens

    Text, plus kinesthetic demonstrations that provide video, arm trajectories, and key joint poses.

When to provide it?

The same textual guidance and robot demonstrations, handed over at the outset or requested by the agent. On request, final success is 86% against 53%, development time falls from 7.2 to 3.7 hours, and output tokens from 1.16 to 0.73 million.

  • Upfront guidance

    53% 7.2 h, 1.16 M tokens

    The agent receives textual guidance and robot demonstrations at the start of development.

  • On-request guidance

    86% 3.7 h, 0.73 M tokens

    The agent decides whether and when to ask, before its first physical attempt or during later revision.

How should simulation come in?

Simulation as a separate stage before transfer, or as a tool the agent calls on during real-world development. Simulation on demand reaches 100% final success against 86% for real-only development, in 2.6 rather than 3.7 hours, with more output tokens.

  • Real-only

    86% 3.7 h, 0.73 M tokens

    Development through real-world execution and feedback, without simulation. Text and robot demonstrations on request.

  • Sequential real-sim-real

    0% 3.5 h, 1.86 M tokens

    Demonstrations first, then a simulation built from the real setup and ten rounds of simulated exploration in four parallel environments, then transfer. The transferred program fails at grasping the plug: it was refined against cleaner simulated images and depth without the real sensor's close-range blind regions.

  • Simulation on demand

    100% 2.6 h, 1.09 M tokens

    Development stays in the real-world loop. The agent decides whether and when to simulate, which task segment and initial state to examine, and which revisions to test. Text and robot demonstrations on request.

Percentages are final real-world success over 30 trials of the frozen program. Development time is elapsed hours, including model calls, simulated experiments, and real-world interaction; tokens are output tokens across all model calls during development. Each condition is a single development run on one task, so the 30 trials measure the reliability of the resulting program, while the repeatability of the development process remains to be established.

One development loop, two sources of experience

The working artifact is a program. The agent starts from a task instruction, observations of the workspace, and the robot's perception and control interfaces, and writes code that specifies action sequences, geometric computations, motion parameters, and gripper commands.

At each step it weighs the current program against the evidence it has and the question that is still open. It can revise the code, request a demonstration, organize a simulated experiment, or run a candidate on the robot. Neither demonstrations nor simulation is required in every iteration.

Demonstrations on request

The agent asks for a demonstration when it judges that seeing how a person performs the task with the robot would help it build or revise the policy. A person physically guides the arms through the task; the agent receives video, recorded arm trajectories, and key joint poses that capture the arm configurations at critical stages, and reads them together. The demonstrated action order and arm roles inform the program's structure; approach directions, motion paths, and key joint poses inform individual actions.

Simulation on demand

A simulated experiment starts from a development question: does an approach path avoid an obstruction, how does a motion parameter change the interaction, in what order should coordinated actions run. The agent chooses the task segment, the initial state, and the candidate changes to test, then interprets the outcome against the question that motivated it. The conclusion applies to the behavior and conditions that experiment examined; physical execution then tests the revision on the robot.

From a parcel-reorientation development log

One evening, two simulated experiments, each answered in about ten minutes. Messages are translated from the Chinese originals and abridged.

  1. On the robot

    The parcel lies very close to the robot, rotated 45°. The program finds no start pose that both hands can reach and stops without moving.

  2. Agent revises the code and asks for an experiment
    Scene: the box lies close to the robot, between the arms, rotated 45°, label not facing up. The program should push the box outwards before turning it, but before the push it finds no grasp both hands can reach (28 candidates rejected), so it stops. Change: when sliding or pushing, also try contact points offset by 3 and 4.5 cm with the hand tilted 15° or 20°; if one pair of faces fails, try the other pair. Please use this box position and angle (x 0.24–0.27 m, 42°–48°, both directions), run the whole flow five times, and check whether the push, turn, flip and pull-back all succeed.
  3. In simulation

    Five initial states run in four parallel digital twins, with the candidate code unchanged and no agent in the loop. The previous code reproduces the real stop. The revision succeeds in four of five; in the fifth the backend refuses a two-arm straight-line move, because the right arm cannot reach the pose 77% of the way along the line.

  4. On the robot

    The next real run meets the same refusal, 65% of the way along the line.

  5. Agent finds the cause

    The right wrist's last joint sits within a few hundredths of a radian of its limit at the pre-pose, while the start pose was solved with the opposite half-turn, so the straight line between them runs into the limit. The fix rejects candidates whose waypoints mix half-turn solutions. Replaying the planner on 674 box measurements recorded on the robot leaves 672 plans unchanged; the other two are this scene.

  6. In simulation

    The same five states again. The previous code reproduces the refusal; the fix succeeds in five of five.

  7. On the robot

    The next real run finds a start pose, pushes the parcel 9.3 cm and turns it, with no refusal. A flip check later in that run failed for an unrelated reason and was revised separately.

The same initial state of bottle uncapping, seen by the real camera and rendered in the twin. The twins mirror the station's kinematics, camera poses, fixed obstacles and safety checks. That makes them good at questions of geometry and control flow like the one above; the reliability of contact, such as a grasp or an insertion, is left to the robot.

The policy is a program

At the end of development the agent produces a condensed code policy that retains the task logic and execution details worked out through interaction, expressed in its action structure, geometric computations, and motion parameters. Once frozen, its decision rules stay fixed, while runtime observations still change the values those rules compute and the actions that result. It executes without a human or coding agent in the loop.

Eight tasks

The tasks span alignment, object reorientation, multi-object handling, and bimanual coordination. They include inserting a charger into a socket, removing a bottle cap while stabilizing the bottle, and placing a pair of shoes into a box.

221 of 240 real-world trials succeed

Each program is developed with at most ten human-assisted scene resets, then frozen and run for 30 trials that are separate from development. Seven of the eight tasks reach at least 90%.

Success rate over 30 evaluation trials, mean physical execution time of the frozen program, and output tokens generated across all model calls during policy development (paper, Table 1 and Fig. 7).

Charger Insertion succeeds in all 30 trials and Chicken Plating in 29. Bread Packing, at 25 of 30, is the one task below 90%. Development uses between 0.75 and 2.14 million output tokens per task.

Mean physical execution times range from 0.97 to 2.27 minutes, and six of the eight tasks average less than two minutes. Parcel Reorientation is the shortest at 0.97 minutes; Bottle Uncapping and Shoe Packing are the longest at 2.23 and 2.27 minutes, both with 90% success.
